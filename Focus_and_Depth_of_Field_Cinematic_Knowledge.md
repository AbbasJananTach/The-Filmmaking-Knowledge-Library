# Focus and Depth-of-Field Design — Cinematic Knowledge Base

## 1. Purpose and Core Principle

Focus controls what appears optically sharp, what falls out of sharpness, and how the viewer's attention moves through the image. In cinema, focus is not simply a technical requirement to make a face clear. It is a storytelling decision that determines which information is available, when the viewer notices it, how spatial relationships are perceived, and whether action remains understandable.

**Core principle:** keep the information sharp that the viewer needs at that moment, and use selective focus only when it strengthens attention, emotion, or spatial meaning. A beautifully blurred background is not useful if it hides the clue, obstacle, threat, or movement that makes the scene understandable.

This knowledge base covers focus as a practical system: focus distance, depth of field, selective focus, focus transitions, lens and aperture relationships, motion, focus continuity, and the special failure modes of AI-generated images and video.

---

## 2. What Focus Means

### 2.1 Focus Plane and Focus Distance
The focus plane is the distance region rendered most sharply by the lens. Focus distance is the distance at which the lens is set to focus. Objects at or near that distance appear sharpest; objects farther in front or behind become progressively less sharp.

The focus plane is not always a literal flat wall across the image in every lens setup, and real lenses have optical complexities, but thinking of a plane or zone is useful for shot planning.

### 2.2 Sharpness Is Gradual
Objects do not instantly switch from perfectly sharp to completely blurred. Sharpness falls off gradually. The region that appears acceptably sharp is called the **depth of field** (DoF). It depends on factors including aperture, focal length, focus distance, sensor or image format, and the final viewing conditions.

### 2.3 Shallow Depth of Field
A shallow depth of field keeps a relatively narrow distance range acceptably sharp. It can isolate a face, hand, clue, or emotional reaction from a busy environment.

**Useful when:** the viewer should concentrate on a specific subject or detail, the background is distracting, or the scene requires intimacy.

**Risk:** it can hide action geography, make a subject's movement drift out of focus, or blur another story-critical element.

### 2.4 Deep Depth of Field
A deep depth of field keeps a broader range of distances acceptably sharp. It can preserve spatial information and allow the viewer to inspect multiple planes of action.

**Useful when:** geography, environmental threat, blocking, or multiple simultaneous actions matter.

**Risk:** a busy background may compete with the subject, and the viewer may not know where to look unless composition, lighting, movement, and color establish hierarchy.

### 2.5 Focus Versus Sharpness
Focus is not identical to overall image sharpness. Motion blur, lens softness, atmospheric haze, compression, sharpening, and subject texture can all change perceived clarity. A face may be technically in focus but still look soft because of motion blur or low contrast. Conversely, aggressive sharpening may make edges look crisp without restoring genuine detail.

## 3. Why Focus Matters to Story

Focus can:

1. **Direct attention** to the face, hand, object, or movement that matters.
2. **Control information** by delaying or revealing details.
3. **Create intimacy** by isolating a small reaction or tactile detail.
4. **Preserve geography** when foreground, subject, and background all need to remain legible.
5. **Communicate attention** by showing what a character is looking at or mentally registering.
6. **Connect story elements** through a deliberate focus transition from one plane to another.
7. **Create tension** by keeping a threat unclear, then allowing it to resolve into view.
8. **Establish distance or observation** through deep focus or long-lens separation.
9. **Support continuity** by maintaining consistent focus behavior between shots.
10. **Expose or conceal visual relationships** between foreground and background.
11. **Make action readable** by ensuring important movement remains within an appropriate sharpness range.
12. **Suggest subjectivity** when focus becomes unstable or selectively sharp during disorientation, fear, or altered perception—provided this treatment is deliberate.

Focus should not be used as a substitute for staging. If the viewer cannot understand where a character is or what action occurred, blurring more of the image may make the problem worse.

## 4. Choose Focus According to Information Priority

Before selecting depth of field, identify the information the viewer must read.

### Primary information
The essential subject or action: a face reacting, a hand grasping a ledge, a foot slipping, a clue being discovered, or a threat entering the frame.

### Secondary information
Useful context that supports the action: nearby terrain, another character, a doorway, an object being approached, or the direction of escape.

### Background information
Environmental context that may establish location, scale, danger, or atmosphere. It may be soft, but it should not be unintentionally unreadable if the story depends on it.

### Focus priority test
Ask:
- What must the viewer see first?
- What must remain understandable at the same time?
- Is a blurred area hiding important cause-and-effect information?
- Would deeper focus improve comprehension more than shallow focus improves mood?
- Can composition, light, or movement guide attention without relying on blur?

**Decision rule:** choose the shallowest depth of field that serves the scene, not the shallowest depth of field possible.

## 5. The Main Factors Affecting Depth of Field

### 5.1 Aperture
A wider aperture (lower f-number, such as f/1.8) generally produces shallower depth of field than a smaller aperture (higher f-number, such as f/8), all else equal. Aperture also changes exposure and may affect optical rendering.

Do not treat a wide aperture as a universal cinematic setting. If the subject moves toward or away from the camera, a very shallow focus zone can become difficult to maintain.

### 5.2 Focal Length
Focal length affects framing and perspective relationships. Under common practical comparisons, a longer lens used to maintain similar subject framing often produces a shallower-looking depth of field and stronger background compression. The exact comparison depends on camera position, magnification, sensor format, and framing.

Do not change focal length only to create blur if it damages perspective, working distance, or spatial understanding.

### 5.3 Focus Distance
Closer focus distances generally produce shallower depth of field at the same aperture and focal length. Close-ups of eyes, hands, or small objects can therefore have a very narrow sharp region.

### 5.4 Sensor or Image Format
Format affects the field of view and depth-of-field relationships for a given framing and camera position. Comparisons must account for equivalent framing, lens, aperture, and distance rather than treating sensor size as an isolated magic control.

### 5.5 Subject Movement
A subject moving through depth may leave the focus zone even if the camera remains still. A character moving laterally across a roughly constant distance may be easier to keep sharp than a character approaching rapidly.

### 5.6 Camera Movement
A push-in, pull-back, orbit, or handheld drift can change the subject-to-camera distance and focus requirements. Focus must be planned across the movement, not only at its first frame.

### 5.7 Final Display and Viewing Conditions
A depth-of-field choice that appears acceptable on a large monitor may feel different on a phone, after compression, or at a different viewing distance. Check the final delivery format and size.

## 6. Focus Modes and Shot Strategies

### 6.1 Fixed Focus
The focus distance remains set while the subject or camera moves only within a range that stays acceptably sharp.

**Use when:** the subject’s distance is stable, the scene is observational, or a controlled composition allows movement within the focus zone.

**Failure:** the subject moves too close or too far and becomes visibly soft.

**Correction:** increase depth of field, change the blocking, adjust camera distance, or use a planned focus pull.

### 6.2 Follow Focus / Continuous Focus
Focus is adjusted to follow a moving subject. In production this may be manual, assisted, or automated.

**Use when:** the subject moves through depth and must remain sharp.

**Failure:** focus hunts, lags, jumps to the background, or alternates between competing subjects.

**Correction:** define the priority subject, reduce unnecessary competing detail, plan movement and focus marks, or use a deeper focus zone when appropriate.

### 6.3 Rack Focus / Focus Pull
The focus deliberately transfers from one subject or plane to another within a shot. A rack focus is a storytelling event, not merely a demonstration of lens capability.

**Use when:** the viewer should discover a clue, notice a threat, or shift attention between characters without a cut.

**Failure:** the focus shift occurs too early, too late, too quickly, or without a meaningful target; the viewer notices the effect rather than the story.

**Correction:** establish the initial focal target, define the reason for the shift, set the destination target, and time the transition to the story beat.

### 6.4 Deep-Focus Staging
Multiple planes remain readable, so the audience can choose where to look or understand simultaneous action.

**Use when:** foreground and background events are narratively connected, the environment is a source of danger, or spatial relationships matter.

**Failure:** the frame becomes visually busy and lacks hierarchy.

**Correction:** use composition, light, movement, color, contrast, and blocking to prioritize information even when it is all sharp.

### 6.5 Selective Focus
One subject or plane is sharp while others are deliberately soft.

**Use when:** attention must be tightly controlled or the scene should feel intimate or subjective.

**Failure:** important environmental information is lost or the subject looks pasted into the frame.

**Correction:** preserve enough background structure, maintain plausible light and lens behavior, and make sure the sharp subject is physically integrated with the scene.

### 6.6 Foreground Obstruction
A soft or sharp foreground object may partially cover the frame. This can create a sense of observation, enclosure, concealment, or physical proximity.

**Use when:** the character is hidden, watched, trapped, or moving through dense space.

**Failure:** obstruction covers the face, hands, clue, or action that must be seen.

**Correction:** move the camera or obstruction, reduce the obstruction’s size, change focus, or use it only at moments when concealment is intentional.

## 7. Focus Pull Design

A focus pull needs a clear narrative plan.

### Before the pull
- Identify what is initially sharp.
- Ensure the viewer has time to register it.
- Establish the second subject or plane in the composition, even if soft.
- Decide what event triggers the shift: a sound, glance, movement, realization, or reveal.

### During the pull
- Define the direction of attention: near to far, far to near, subject to object, or one character to another.
- Choose a speed that fits the story beat.
- Preserve enough motion and framing stability for the shift to read.
- Avoid a mechanically perfect transition if the scene’s style calls for imperfect, human-operated focus.

### After the pull
- Hold the new focal target long enough for comprehension.
- Ensure the target is the intended object or person.
- Check that the original subject does not remain confusingly important while blurred.
- Make sure the focus shift does not accidentally reveal information too early.

### Timing principles
- A quick pull can signal sudden recognition or urgency.
- A slow pull can create anticipation or gradual discovery.
- A pull triggered by a character’s gaze can connect perception and attention.
- A pull without a clear destination usually feels ornamental.

The pull’s speed should follow the psychological and narrative beat, not a preset preference.

## 8. Focus, Lens Perspective, and Framing

Focus and lens choice must be designed together.

### Close-up
Close focus often creates shallow depth of field. Decide whether both eyes, the whole face, or only a detail should be sharp. A very thin focus plane may cause one eye to be sharp while the other becomes noticeably soft. This can be expressive, but should not occur accidentally when the scene requires a clear facial read.

### Wide shot
A wide shot often needs enough depth of field to preserve spatial relationships. Shallow focus can be used in a wide shot, but it may make the environment feel less informative or the subject oddly isolated.

### Wide-angle lens
A wide lens can keep more of the environment in view and create a sense of physical proximity. At a given framing, focus distance and aperture still affect depth of field. Close wide-angle framing can distort facial features and body proportions.

### Telephoto lens
A longer lens can isolate a subject and compress apparent space. Focus may be more demanding when depth of field is shallow, and background objects can appear visually close to the subject.

### Macro or detail shot
Very close focus may produce extremely shallow depth of field. If a small object’s important features must be read, use sufficient focus depth, a suitable angle, and controlled movement.

**Rule:** choose the lens and camera position for the desired perspective and spatial relationship first; then choose the focus strategy that supports it.

## 9. Focus and Character Psychology

Focus can reflect attention, but it should not be used as a simplistic emotion code.

- **Calm observation:** stable focus and measured transitions can support control.
- **Sudden recognition:** a purposeful rack focus or shift in focal priority can mark the discovery.
- **Disorientation:** temporary focus instability may suggest perceptual disturbance, but it can also look like a technical mistake.
- **Fear:** selective focus may isolate the character, while a wider readable frame may make the surrounding threat more frightening.
- **Intimacy:** close focus on eyes, hands, breath, or a small gesture can make a moment feel private.
- **Isolation:** a sharp subject in a soft environment can separate a character from the world.
- **Awareness of danger:** shifting focus from the character to an approaching object can show what becomes important.

Do not automatically blur the world whenever a character is afraid. In many suspense scenes, keeping the environment sharp is more threatening because the audience can inspect it.

## 10. Focus and Suspense / Information Control

Focus is useful for revealing and withholding information, but it should respect the scene’s logic.

### Concealment
A soft background can hide a threat, but blur does not guarantee concealment. Silhouette, movement, color, sound, or an obvious shape may still reveal the object.

### Discovery
A focus pull can reveal a detail already present in the frame. The composition should allow the viewer to understand where the new target is located.

### Uncertainty
The viewer may be uncertain about what an object is, but should not be confused about the basic geography unless confusion is intentionally part of the experience.

### Suspense rule
Keep the viewer uncertain about the right information, not unable to understand the image. If the character’s danger depends on an object in the background, decide whether the audience should see it clearly, partially, or only after a trigger.

## 11. Focus Continuity Across Cuts

Focus continuity is not just whether every shot is sharp. It concerns whether sharpness and attention behave coherently.

Track:
- which character or object is the primary focal target;
- the approximate focus distance and depth-of-field character;
- whether the shot uses deep or shallow focus;
- which eyes or object features must remain sharp;
- whether the focus shifts during the action;
- how the background is rendered;
- the relationship between focus and camera movement;
- whether the next shot begins with a new focal priority;
- whether the focus change is a deliberate attention shift or an accidental mismatch.

### Common continuity issue
A character’s face is sharply isolated in one shot, but the reverse shot suddenly has the entire background equally sharp without a motivated lens, distance, or aperture change. This may feel like an unintended change in visual style.

### Matching workflow
1. Establish the focal priority for each shot.
2. Decide whether the shot should feel shallow, moderate, or deep.
3. Match the intended style across coverage.
4. Allow changes when shot size, distance, lens, or story function justifies them.
5. Review adjacent shots in sequence.
6. Check whether the audience’s attention transfers naturally across the cut.

Do not force identical blur in every shot. Continuity means coherent choices, not mechanical sameness.

## 12. Focus in Moving Shots

A moving shot has changing focus requirements.

### Push-in
As the camera approaches a subject, focus distance changes. A planned push-in may require a focus adjustment or a depth of field large enough to tolerate the move.

### Pull-back
As the camera moves away, the focus distance changes in the opposite direction. Background context may become more important as the frame widens.

### Tracking alongside a subject
If subject and camera maintain roughly the same distance, focus may remain stable, but small changes in path, speed, or body movement can still cause drift.

### Orbit
An orbit changes the background relationship and may expose objects at different distances. Focus must remain on the intended subject unless a shift is planned.

### Handheld movement
Small camera movements can change the distance to a close subject and make shallow focus unstable. Handheld style should not automatically mean soft focus.

### Fast action
Focus strategy must prioritize action comprehension. A little extra depth of field may be more valuable than extreme background blur.

**Planning rule:** define the subject’s path, camera path, focus target, and acceptable sharpness range together.

## 13. Common Focus Failure Modes and Fixes

### Failure 1 — Eyes are not sharp
**Symptoms:** face looks soft even though the rest of the image is clear.  
**Likely causes:** focus landed on nose, hair, ears, or background; motion blur; extremely shallow depth of field.  
**Fix:** prioritize the intended eye or eye region, increase focus tolerance, reduce movement, or use a slightly deeper focus range.

### Failure 2 — Focus hunts or pulses
**Symptoms:** sharpness repeatedly shifts between face and background.  
**Likely causes:** competing details, poor subject tracking, low contrast, or automated focus behavior.  
**Fix:** clarify the primary target, simplify competing visual detail, stabilize movement, or use planned focus transitions.

### Failure 3 — Focus shifts to the wrong subject
**Symptoms:** background foliage or a passing object becomes sharp while the protagonist softens.  
**Likely causes:** ambiguous priority or visual competition.  
**Fix:** define the subject as the persistent priority unless a deliberate rack focus is required; reduce competing contrast or movement.

### Failure 4 — Excessive shallow focus
**Symptoms:** only a tiny part of the face is sharp; hands and action disappear into blur.  
**Likely causes:** shallow depth of field chosen as a generic cinematic effect.  
**Fix:** increase depth of field, adjust framing or distance, or use focus pulls only when they serve the story.

### Failure 5 — Background is too sharp and distracting
**Symptoms:** the viewer looks at foliage, signs, or bright objects instead of the subject.  
**Likely causes:** deep focus without a hierarchy.  
**Fix:** simplify composition, control light and color, change angle, use moderate depth of field, or reduce background detail.

### Failure 6 — Rack focus feels pointless
**Symptoms:** focus moves but the viewer learns nothing.  
**Likely causes:** no clear target or narrative trigger.  
**Fix:** connect the shift to a sound, gaze, discovery, or change in importance; hold the destination.

### Failure 7 — Focus pull is too slow or too fast
**Symptoms:** the reveal is missed, or the effect becomes showy.  
**Likely causes:** timing chosen independently of the story beat.  
**Fix:** align the pull with recognition, suspense, or action timing and verify at normal playback speed.

### Failure 8 — Focus breaks during camera movement
**Symptoms:** subject becomes soft during a push-in, orbit, or tracking shot.  
**Likely causes:** focus plan ignores the movement path.  
**Fix:** map the subject-to-camera distance, use more depth of field, simplify the camera path, or plan focus adjustment.

### Failure 9 — Sharpness changes randomly between generated shots
**Symptoms:** one angle has very shallow blur; the next looks like a phone snapshot; another has a blurred face.  
**Likely causes:** inconsistent prompt instructions or model variation.  
**Fix:** specify the focus priority and depth-of-field character per shot, while keeping the global style consistent. Inspect and regenerate or edit only the affected shot.

### Failure 10 — Artificial blur or cutout edges
**Symptoms:** subject appears pasted onto a blurry background; hair and shoulders have strange blur halos.  
**Likely causes:** synthetic depth-map errors, excessive background blur, or poor subject separation.  
**Fix:** reduce blur, preserve natural edge transitions, use a more plausible depth-of-field description, and avoid demanding extreme bokeh in complex foliage.

### Failure 11 — Focus obscures the clue
**Symptoms:** an object is too soft to identify when the story requires recognition.  
**Likely causes:** concealment prioritized over comprehension.  
**Fix:** choose the intended reveal time, ensure the object becomes sufficiently sharp, and hold long enough for the viewer to recognize it.

### Failure 12 — Every shot uses the same shallow focus
**Symptoms:** visual monotony and poor geography.  
**Likely causes:** a single “cinematic” setting applied universally.  
**Fix:** use shallow focus for selective attention, moderate depth for interaction, and deep focus when spatial information matters.

### Failure 13 — Focus does not match the character’s gaze
**Symptoms:** character looks toward a clue, but the clue remains visually unimportant or another object is sharp.  
**Likely causes:** gaze and focal priority were planned separately.  
**Fix:** coordinate eyeline, composition, and focus transition. A focus shift is optional; a cut to the object may be clearer.

### Failure 14 — Motion blur is mistaken for missed focus
**Symptoms:** a moving hand or face appears soft despite the focus plane being correct.  
**Likely causes:** motion blur, shutter behavior, or excessive movement.  
**Fix:** distinguish blur caused by motion from optical defocus before changing focus distance. Reduce motion blur only if it improves the intended style and action readability.

## 14. Focus in AI Image and Video Prompts

A strong focus instruction describes the intended viewer experience and the objects that must remain readable.

### Weak instruction
“Shallow depth of field, cinematic bokeh, sharp focus, ultra-realistic.”

Problems:
- “shallow depth of field” conflicts with “sharp focus” if the intended sharp region is not specified;
- no focal target is named;
- no focus behavior is defined during movement;
- background blur may hide story-critical information;
- the system may create artificial blur halos.

### Stronger prompt structure
1. **Primary focal target:** exact person, eye region, hand, prop, or action.
2. **Secondary readable information:** identify what must also remain understandable.
3. **Depth-of-field level:** shallow, moderate, or deep, with story reason.
4. **Focus behavior:** fixed, following, or deliberate rack focus.
5. **Movement plan:** how focus should respond to camera and subject movement.
6. **Background behavior:** how soft or readable the background should be.
7. **Transition timing:** where the focus pull begins and ends.
8. **Continuity locks:** preserve focus style across related shots.
9. **Failure prevention:** avoid wrong focal targets, focus hunting, artificial blur, or hiding clues.
10. **Verification:** check eyes, hands, key objects, and action at normal viewing size.

### Example prompt: intimate close-up
“Keep the heroine’s nearer eye and the eye region in clear, natural focus, with realistic skin texture. Use a moderate-to-shallow depth of field so the jungle softens without becoming an artificial blur wall. Preserve enough background shape to communicate that she is under dense canopy. Keep focus stable through the small head movement; do not let sharpness jump to leaves behind her. Avoid waxy skin, blur halos around hair, and exaggerated bokeh.”

### Example prompt: discovering a threat
“Begin with the heroine’s face as the primary focal target in a medium shot. A faint movement occurs in the background, initially soft but spatially recognizable. As her gaze shifts toward it, perform one deliberate, smooth focus pull to the moving shape. Hold the new focus long enough for the audience to register the threat, while retaining enough environmental context to understand its distance. Do not reveal more detail than the scene intends, and do not shift focus to unrelated foliage.”

### Prompt discipline
- Name what must be sharp.
- State what can be soft and why.
- Avoid asking for the entire frame to be both extremely blurred and fully sharp.
- Specify the focus change only if it contributes to the story.
- Keep focus instructions compatible with camera distance and movement.
- For action, prioritize readability over decorative blur.
- Treat generated focus behavior as something to inspect, not a guarantee.

## 15. Interaction With Other Cinematic Elements

### Subject
The subject’s eyes, hands, expression, and action may require different focus priorities. Decide which carries the story beat.

### Scene
A scene can move from environmental awareness to a close emotional reaction. Focus may evolve with that shift, but the change should be motivated.

### Composition
Composition determines where the viewer looks; focus reinforces or changes that hierarchy. If focus and composition point to different things accidentally, attention becomes confused.

### Lighting
Contrast and light can direct attention without blur. A focused face in flat light may not stand out if a bright background competes with it.

### Color
A saturated object can attract attention even when out of focus. Focus is not the only attention-control system.

### Camera Movement
Movement changes distances and may require focus adjustment. Plan the camera and focus together.

### Lens Choice
Lens choice affects perspective, framing, and the practical depth-of-field result. Do not choose a lens only for blur.

### Editing
A focus pull can replace a cut in some situations, while a cut can provide clearer information in others. Choose the simplest method that supports the intended reveal.

### Mood
Shallow focus can create intimacy or isolation, while deep focus can expose the character to a readable environment. Neither effect has a fixed emotional meaning.

### Sound
An off-screen sound may trigger a gaze and then a focus shift, but the sequence should be timed so the viewer can follow the relationship between sound, reaction, and reveal.

## 16. Decision Procedure for Any Shot

Use this sequence before finalizing focus:

1. **Story purpose:** What information should the viewer notice now?
2. **Primary target:** Which face, eye region, hand, object, or action must be sharp?
3. **Secondary information:** What else must remain readable?
4. **Spatial requirement:** Does the audience need to understand foreground, subject, and background relationships?
5. **Depth choice:** Should the shot use shallow, moderate, or deep depth of field?
6. **Lens and distance:** Does the chosen framing create a practical focus range?
7. **Movement:** Will the subject or camera move through depth?
8. **Focus mode:** Fixed focus, follow focus, or a planned rack focus?
9. **Timing:** If focus changes, what triggers it and how long should the new target be held?
10. **Attention conflicts:** Are bright colors, contrast, or movement attracting attention away from the focal target?
11. **Continuity:** Does focus behavior match adjacent shots unless a change is motivated?
12. **Failure prediction:** Could the eyes, hands, clue, or action become soft at a critical moment?
13. **Verification:** Review the full shot at normal speed and final delivery size.
14. **Smallest effective correction:** Fix the cause without adding unnecessary blur or camera complexity.

---

## 17. Quality-Control Checklist

Before approving a shot or prompt, verify:

- [ ] The intended focal target is explicit.
- [ ] The focus choice supports the story’s information hierarchy.
- [ ] Depth of field is appropriate to the shot size, distance, lens, and movement.
- [ ] The viewer can understand important action and spatial relationships.
- [ ] The eyes or other critical details are sharp when required.
- [ ] Background blur is not hiding necessary story information.
- [ ] Deep focus still has a clear visual hierarchy.
- [ ] Rack focus has a meaningful starting point, destination, trigger, and duration.
- [ ] Focus remains stable during planned camera and subject movement.
- [ ] Focus behavior is coherent across adjacent shots.
- [ ] Color and contrast do not unintentionally overpower the focal target.
- [ ] Blur around hair, limbs, and environmental edges looks natural.
- [ ] Motion blur has not been confused with defocus.
- [ ] The result has been checked at normal playback speed and final screen size.
- [ ] Any focus change is motivated rather than included as decoration.

## 18. Worked Example — A Stranded Heroine Hears Something in the Jungle

### Story situation
The heroine is wet, exhausted, and listening in a dense jungle. The audience should share her attention but still understand that the environment surrounds her.

### Focus plan
- **Opening wide shot:** use moderate-to-deep focus so the audience understands her location and nearby escape routes.
- **Medium reaction shot:** keep her face and eyes sharp, with enough background detail to preserve spatial context.
- **Sound trigger:** she stops and looks toward a gap in the foliage. Do not immediately reveal the threat.
- **Focus transition:** if the threat is already visible in the frame, shift focus toward the moving shape only when the story intends the audience to notice it.
- **Hold:** keep the new focal target readable long enough to identify its movement, without revealing more than intended.
- **Follow-up:** return to the heroine’s reaction or cut to a new angle according to the scene’s information needs.

### Why not use extreme blur?
Extreme shallow focus may remove the very foliage movement that creates suspense. The audience may see only a face and have no spatial sense of where the threat could be. Moderate depth of field may be more effective because the viewer can search the environment.

### Example AI prompt
“Naturalistic jungle-survival shot. Begin with the heroine’s face and eyes in clear focus while retaining enough detail in the surrounding foliage to establish depth and possible movement. Use moderate depth of field rather than extreme cinematic blur. When she hears a sound and looks toward a gap in the leaves, make one deliberate focus transition only if the moving shape is present in the same frame; hold the new focal target long enough to register its movement. Preserve natural skin texture, realistic lens behavior, and stable character identity. Avoid focus hunting, random background sharpness changes, artificial blur halos, and blur that hides the important action.”

### Verification questions
- Can the viewer understand where she is looking?
- Is the potential threat visible at the intended moment—not earlier or later?
- Does the focus change have a clear target?
- Does the frame retain enough environmental information for suspense?
- Does the shot remain coherent with the project’s restrained visual style?

## 19. Trade-Offs and Advanced Judgment

### Isolation versus geography
Shallow focus isolates a subject but reduces spatial information. Use it when isolation is more important than geography; use deeper focus when the relationship between subject and environment matters.

### Attention control versus viewer freedom
Selective focus tells the viewer where to look. Deep focus allows the viewer to search the frame. Choose based on whether the scene should guide attention tightly or let the audience discover details.

### Blur versus composition
Blur can reduce distraction, but composition, lighting, color, and blocking may solve the problem more naturally. Avoid treating blur as the default fix for a messy frame.

### Focus pull versus cut
A focus pull preserves the continuous spatial relationship between two targets. A cut can make the target clearer and give the audience a new composition. Use a focus pull when the relationship within one frame matters; cut when a new angle provides better comprehension.

### Precision versus spontaneity
Highly controlled focus can feel elegant and deliberate. Imperfect focus can support a raw observational style, but missed focus should not be mistaken for authenticity when it hides the story.

### Optical realism versus generated approximation
AI-generated blur may not obey true lens geometry. The goal is a plausible and coherent visual result, not merely a large blur radius. Inspect edges, subject depth, and transitions.

## 20. Operating Rules for a Cinematic AI Skill

When designing, generating, or evaluating focus, the skill should:

1. Determine the story information priority before choosing depth of field.
2. Identify the exact primary focal target and any secondary details that must remain readable.
3. Choose shallow, moderate, or deep focus according to spatial and emotional needs.
4. Consider lens, camera distance, aperture-like rendering, subject movement, and camera movement together.
5. Use rack focus only when a meaningful attention transfer occurs.
6. Coordinate focus with gaze, composition, lighting, color, sound, and editing.
7. Preserve action geography when physical movement or danger depends on it.
8. Maintain coherent focus behavior across related shots without forcing every shot to look identical.
9. Diagnose defocus, motion blur, artificial blur, and low contrast separately.
10. Predict likely focus failures before generating the shot.
11. Correct the smallest relevant cause and recheck downstream effects.
12. Evaluate the full shot at normal speed, not only a still frame.
13. Avoid shallow depth of field as a generic synonym for “cinematic.”
14. Treat AI-generated focus behavior as an intention requiring visual verification.
15. Explain trade-offs when both shallow and deep focus are plausible but serve different story purposes.

**Final principle:** focus is a decision about attention and information. The best focus is not the blurriest background or the sharpest possible frame; it is the focus behavior that lets the audience see the right thing at the right moment.
